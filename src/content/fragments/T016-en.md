# [Work Diary](/fragments/A035)

2025.01.23  
  
I didn’t know X, who worked in the kitchen of the ramen restaurant, very well. Once, when the restaurant was quiet, I went outside for a cigarette and happened to run into him. We said hello.  
He was from Northeast China.  
  
“Smoking isn’t good for girls,” he said.  
“You smoke too,” I replied.  
He said he smoked because he was bored. Apart from working every day, there was nothing else for him to do.  
“Isn’t Shenyang pretty good?” I asked. “What did you come to Germany for?”  
“To make money. What else?”  
He said he still thought it was good to read a little—to have an inner life of one’s own.  
I said reading too many books wasn’t good either.  
Later, he gave me [a pack of Nanjing cigarettes](/fragments/F274/). It made me ridiculously happy.  

At around eight this evening, he asked whether I could go with him to get a CT scan the next morning, since he spoke neither German nor English.  
“My German isn’t very good,” I said.  
“English is fine too.”  
Seeing me hesitate, he added, “If you have a friend who could help, you can ask them. I can pay.”  
I thought about it.  
“At this hour, my German friends probably won’t reply.”  
“A Chinese friend would be fine too.”  
For a moment, my mind twisted itself into a knot of mush. I ran through everyone I knew and realized there really was no one I could ask.  
Suddenly, I felt a little sad.  
I asked where the hospital was. It was quite far from my place—almost an hour by tram. His appointment was at eight in the morning.
I thought: There’s no way I’ll be able to get up.  
Without thinking, I turned him down.  
“That’s too early. I won’t be able to get up.”  
He went to ask the other people working at the restaurant.  
  
After refusing him, I paced back and forth through the restaurant for a long time, overwhelmed by wave after wave of emotion.
Not long before, I too had spent much of my time seeing doctors. That sense of helplessness, mixed with the smell of miso soup, rose into my nostrils.  
  
I went back into the kitchen to find him.  
“I’ll go with you tomorrow,” I said. “I’ll just have to get up sometime after six.”  
Then I asked him for the exact address.  
By the time I finished work, I was starving. He loaded my plate with food.  
  
  


2025.01.24 
   
On our way to the hospital, X told me about his grand plan for saving money.  
“One person can save more than two thousand euros a month,” he said. “If you have a partner, the two of you can save six thousand together.”  
“That’s true. It’s hard being on your own in Germany,” I said. “Ah, fuck it—we’ll muddle through somehow.”  
We both burst out laughing.  
“Exactly,” he said. “Everyone else manages to live just fine. So can we.”  
Before the CT scan, he told me to smoke less.  
“I can’t quit right now,” I said. “I’ll try to keep it to three or five a day.”  
  
When we left the hospital, he handed me another cigarette.  
Outside a Turkish breakfast café, I said, “Next time you feel like smoking, memorize some German words instead.”  
  
After we left the café, he handed me thirty euros.  
“What are you giving me this for?”  
“I know things aren’t easy for you either.”  
I didn’t refuse.  
“All right,” I said. “I’ll take it as lucky money for the New Year.”  
  



2025.08.02  
  
That afternoon, I watched Youth (Hard Times), the second film in Wang Bing’s Youth trilogy. I felt drowsy. The weather had been awful lately—either overcast or raining.  
I hurriedly packed the chopped chillies and pickled mustard tuber I wanted to give C. I was going back to China for two months and was afraid they would spoil if I left them for too long.  
As I stepped outside, a tram happened to arrive.  
In my head, I was still thinking about the young man from Anhui who had been beaten by his boss after failing to collect a debt because he couldn’t find the bill.  

This was my second-to-last day working at the noodle restaurant. A few days earlier, I had told the manager that I would leave after this weekend.  

A new Black employee started today. They kept making mistakes while taking orders, and the manager tore into them. Everyone nearby silently lowered their heads.  
There was also a new cook in the kitchen whom I had never seen before—a chubby, good-natured-looking guy. When I carried some plates into the kitchen, he stared at me for a long time before finally managing a solemn:  
“Hello.”  
I froze, unsure how to respond to this sudden politeness. It had been a long time since anyone at the noodle restaurant had greeted me as if they meant it.  
Imitating his tone, I replied with equal solemnity:  
“Hello.”  
  
The dishwasher from Northeast China, who was about my father’s age, said, “If there’s any fried chicken left later, I’ll save a few pieces for you.”  
I thanked him, just as solemnly.  
  
A Ukrainian woman had also just started working as a dishwasher. She was on a video call with a man holding a gun. He appeared to be her husband.  
The dishwasher from Northeast China pointed at the screen.  
“Look, that’s a real gun! He’s going to the battlefield tomorrow.”  
I froze again, not quite understanding what he meant.  
A while later, on my second trip to the kitchen, I asked him, “Was that a video call just now?”  
“Yeah. Her husband is going to the front. She doesn’t even know whether she’ll see him again after tomorrow.”  
I looked over at the woman and nodded to her, trying to convey my respect. She didn’t understand English, so I could only remain silent.  
  
I ate the fried chicken the dishwasher had saved for me. The new cook had just finished cooking a large tray of food.  
“Here,” he said. “Try some of this good stuff.”  
“Can I take one with my fingers?” I asked cautiously.  
“Of course.”  
“The manager scolded me for doing that before.”  
“It’s fine. He’s not here now.”  
His accent had a strong Cantonese lilt, so I casually asked, “Are you from Guangdong?”  
“Macau.”  
“Wow. Do you speak Portuguese?”  
He counted on his fingers.  
“Portuguese, [Cantonese](/fragments/A010/), German, Mandarin. I learned Mandarin over here.”  
Then he asked, “Haven’t I seen you before? I work at Branch No. 4.”  
“I don’t think so. This is my last week working here.”  
“Where are you going?”  
“Back to China for two months.”  
“Where are you from?”  
“Changsha, Hunan.”  
“Oh, Hunan.”  
“Who cut your hair?”  
“I cut it myself.”  
“Why did you cut it like that? It looks as if you lost a bet.”  
Everyone in the kitchen laughed.  
“I’m going back to China. I’m angry with Germany. And [summer is here](/fragments/A019/).”  
“I was going to say—your hair certainly stands out.”
